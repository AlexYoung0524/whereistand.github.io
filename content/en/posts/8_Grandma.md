Before my parents came to visit me in Sweden this July, I made a point of video-calling Lao Yang, my dad, and asking him to bring every old photo he had so I could put them all in the cloud. I suppose I'm getting older. Old things have started to feel precious, I want to hold on to them, and I've begun to feel a small sense of duty about passing memories down. As *Coco* taught us, you only truly disappear when no one in the world remembers you anymore. Speaking of photos, I've always wanted to have AI restore some of the old ones and colorize the black-and-white ones. But I don't know how Lao Yang would feel seeing his mother, or relatives from even further back, suddenly in color. That's probably something I should do on my own, without making a thing of it.

---

I always loved going to Grandma's. Part of it was just a city boy's fascination with the countryside. After a life of spring mattresses, sleeping on a *kang*, the heated brick bed of Manchurian farmhouses, felt like an adventure. Then there was the huge yard right outside the door, the chickens, ducks, geese and pigs, and getting to collect the eggs and feed the pigs myself. Before my family owned a car, I could also climb into the driver's seat of a tractor that wasn't running, spin the steering wheel, yank the gearshift and flick the turn signals while making strange engine noises. For a boy, that's as good as being handed a BB gun or a slingshot. And as the youngest boy in the family, playing with three older cousins was pure happiness. I was the one they had to let win, so I got all kinds of treatment I never got at home. The first time I ever touched a Subor, the Chinese knockoff of the Nintendo, was in the village. I'd never even seen one before. There were also plenty of strange "games" I only ever played at Grandma's, games that sound mind-numbingly dull now but that I loved back then: pushing an old tire around the village, a kind of upgrade on the old hoop-and-stick game; carrying a little lantern my oldest cousin made me out of a clear glass jar; and at New Year, pulling single firecrackers off a string and using them to blast plastic pill bottles into the air to see whose went highest.

But there's no getting around my feelings for Grandma herself. I can no longer say where they came from. I only remember thinking, very early on, that she was someone truly formidable. Not formidable as in strict with me. Formidable as in tough, responsible, and quick and capable at everything she did.

I can't remember Grandma's name anymore, and I have no idea when she was born. She could read, which was rare for a woman her age. I think Lao Yang still has her old schoolbooks; I remember seeing them once. I never met my grandfather. He died around the time I was born. He rarely shows up in Lao Yang's stories either. All I know is that he probably fought in a war and served in the army, and later had a steady job in town, which he eventually passed down to my eldest uncle. That job came with a "red book," a non-farming household registration, which meant a real salary and ration coupons instead of laboring for work points in the village production team. Only recently did my mom tell me that my grandfather was a gambler and lost most of his pay before it ever made it home, which is why Lao Yang hates gambling so much. It also explains why, whenever Lao Yang talks about life before college, it's always about how hard Grandma worked, how carefully she counted every penny to raise three boys and a girl. As Lao Yang sees it, the food was bad, but they never went hungry, there was meat on the table for the holidays, and they never once owed *jihuang*, a word that literally means "famine" but in the local dialect of Manchuria means debt. To him, that was already a lot, and he still sounds proud whenever he brings it up. And it's true, I can't imagine a man as proud and stubborn as him standing at someone's door with a bowl, asking to borrow grain.

Looking back now, when I visited as a kid, Grandma was a bit like a slightly see-through person, someone with an endless list of chores. Back then they still lived in the old house, and if I went alone for summer or winter break, there were just five of us: me, Grandma, my Second Uncle, his wife, and my oldest cousin. My cousin and I slept on the same *kang* as Grandma. I never knew what time she got up. Some days I'd wake up around seven or eight, and by then she'd probably already hauled in the firewood, made breakfast, mixed the pig feed and fed the animals. Then she'd come back in, sit on the edge of the *kang*, and drink a bowl of egg water. I'd never seen anyone eat an egg like that, but Lao Yang says she always did it: crack an egg into a bowl, pour hot water over it, stir, and down it in one go.

I don't remember much about what she did the rest of the day. More bustling around the house and yard, I think. The loudest voice in the house was definitely Second Uncle's. He'd been running the household for years and was some kind of village official back then, and at mealtimes he'd be fussing over everyone, so it was always lively. In winter, Grandma might sit on the *kang* and play some sort of "fortune-telling" game with me using mahjong cards, or watch TV with us, or sit there and smoke. Basically, as long as she was healthy, she was working, and I never saw anyone pay her much attention. Then again, she didn't want anyone looking after her. What I heard most was: Mom, come eat, stop fussing.

---

What I remember more is the time after she hurt her leg. I've forgotten exactly when it happened, probably while I was in elementary school, in 2002 or 2003. The cause had a fated quality to it. She wanted to pick up a single piece of firewood from the neighbors' backyard, and there was a mud wall that came up to about her knee, maybe her thigh at most. She didn't make it over, fell, and injured her hip. My first thought at the time was, oh no, how embarrassing, how is she ever going to face the neighbors? Nobody else seemed to care about that part. I vaguely remember her lying on the *kang* for a very long time. They said she was too old for surgery, so all she could do was rest. She lay at the far end of the *kang*, her leg propped at just the right angle so it wouldn't hurt, with a stack of bricks under her feet.

That's around when I started hearing her mutter, every time I visited, that she'd be better off dead, that this was torture. Lao Yang would smile and tell her, it's fine, you'll get better. Later my mom told me that during that time, Lao Yang went to every hospital and pharmacy he could find, asking what could be done, then bought all kinds of medical equipment and shipped it back to the village. That's when I learned you could send things from Changchun by putting them on a long-distance bus, and someone on the other end would wait at the bus stop to pick them up.

Grandma used to keep her hair in a short, chin-length bob, already streaked with gray. Later it got cut shorter and shorter, until it looked like a man's haircut, and eventually it went completely white. "Lying on the *kang* with long white hair, you look like a madwoman." That's the reason someone gave me. Later, once she was a little better, Lao Yang bought her a walker, the kind you push in front of you, and she slowly learned to walk with it. The upside was that she never had to work again. Now it was entirely other people taking care of her. Even her egg water was made for her and set down beside her. Second Uncle and his wife used to go out drinking and playing mahjong and leave Grandma home alone, but after that, someone always had to stay with her. At first they may have taken turns. Then, over the years, Second Uncle drank harder and harder and his eyesight got worse and worse, so it was always him who stayed home, until in the end he was nearly blind himself.

In a way, I think this was fate. She'd worked too hard her whole life and never let herself enjoy anything when she could have, so maybe heaven finally stepped in and made her rest. From then on, she always sat in the seat of honor on the *kang* at meals. No more finding a spot to squeeze in, or slipping off to eat in the outer room, which in those farmhouses is also the kitchen. At every meal, everyone would ask, Mom, want some of this? How about that? Little by little she accepted it, let people put food in her bowl with a clear conscience, and slowly put on weight. I think she made peace with herself after that. On nice days she'd push her walker out into the yard to sit in the sun. She probably never went beyond the yard again, but I heard that once, later on, she pushed her walker all the way to someone else's house for a visit. Good for her.

Even after she could get around with the walker, she still couldn't get into our Jetta. It sat too low. Lao Yang must have thought long and hard about it, and in the end he had the driver from his work unit bring her to Changchun in the unit's MPV, or maybe it was an SUV. As far as I remember, she only came to Changchun twice. The other time was earlier, when we still lived on Qingnian Road. A few years later, once she was stable and could get into a regular car, Lao Yang drove her all over: to my eldest uncle's in another village, and maybe even farther, to see her sisters who were still alive.

---

When I think about why, of all my elders, I liked Grandma, I think it's because she was genuinely sensible and down-to-earth, without all the stale old ideas, like favoring boys over girls. I don't know how she came to be that way. My maternal grandfather, on the other hand, was a so-called intellectual, and even as a kid I could feel how much he favored boys. It really made me sneer at intellectuals.

To this day, the strangest thing, and the one I regret most, is that we never took a family photo with Grandma. It must have been New Year 2020. For some reason I can't explain, I wanted Lao Yang to bring his DSLR so we could take a picture that year. I did talk him into bringing it. But there were so many people around at New Year, and asking for it felt weird and embarrassing, so I never said anything before we left. Then for New Year 2021, Chengdu wasn't letting people leave because of COVID, and I couldn't go back. Not long after that New Year, she was gone. My mom told me what happened, but I don't remember the details anymore. I think it was when Lao Yang and my mom were about to drive home: heavy snow had closed the village roads, which held them up two more days, and they had only just left, I think they were still on the road, when word came that she wasn't going to make it. I still can't explain why I felt so sure, that one time, that we had to take a picture. And that really was my last chance.

All in all, she was lucky, I suppose. She got to see her great-grandson, and I don't think she suffered at the end. And it was a close call, too. If it had been a few years later, my eldest uncle would have gone before her.

Going through the photos Lao Yang brought this time, I picked out a few with Grandma in them. She was still quick on her feet then, with a hairpin in her hair, and clothes washed until they'd faded but spotlessly clean. Her children stand behind her, and she sits front and center, looking terribly stiff. It's nothing like the old, gentle grandma I remember from later on. But I miss the light in her eyes back then.
